# Hypothesis

Make assumptions explicit and use them to support the current judgment. A hypothesis can be a provisional premise for work, a possible explanation of observations, or a hypothetical condition for exploring consequences.

## Form hypotheses

1. Read the relevant material and separate observations, user-confirmed goals and constraints, recorded assumptions, and your own inferences. Check facts available in the current sources.
2. Surface implicit premises behind the interpretation of the goal, choice of approach, execution arrangements, and conclusions. Where information is missing, propose candidates grounded in the available evidence. Prioritize premises whose failure would change feasibility, a decision, or the work to do.
3. Write consequential hypotheses into the current activity's document within its writing permission. Use an existing document when it fits; create one when the activity authorizes new material. For a conversation-only request, state the candidates in the answer. Resolve permission before expanding the writing scope.
4. Make each candidate understandable: what is being assumed, its basis or source, and the judgment or arrangement that depends on it. Add relevant conditions, evidence gaps, or a useful check at the depth the decision needs. Mark reconstructed premises about past behavior as your hypotheses and cite the record that suggested them.
5. Give the person a draft they can accept, correct, or supplement. Group questions that need their experience, preferences, or decision. Record acceptance of a working premise separately from evidence for its truth. Keep confirmed goals and constraints distinct.

Use a short paragraph or list when sufficient. Scale the record to the consequence of being wrong.

## Use hypotheses

Choose the uses needed for the current activity:

- **Planning:** connect proposed arrangements to the premises they depend on. Identify conditions that need checking before dependent work, and explain what would change if a premise failed.
- **Reflection:** reconstruct the premises available at the time and compare them with actual outcomes. Separate assumptions recorded then from explanations proposed afterward. State the conditions under which a lesson applies, including conditions that contributed to success.
- **Diagnosis:** propose competing explanations, derive observations that would support or weaken each, and select checks that distinguish them. Update the hypotheses from the observed results. The diagnostic activity owns the investigation and its stopping conditions.
- **Hypothetical reasoning:** state the given conditions, derive their consequences, and vary important premises to compare conclusions. Keep those conclusions conditional on their premises.

When evidence or a person's correction changes a hypothesis, update its written account and revisit dependent conclusions and arrangements. State which judgment changed and why. Preserve the distinction between an accepted working premise, evidence supporting an explanation, and a condition introduced for discussion.

The current activity owns record placement, execution, and permissions. Use its workflow for checks and decisions.

## Completion

Finish when the important hypotheses are written clearly enough for review and the requested use has informed the current judgment. Keep consequential unknowns with the decision they affect and the evidence or person needed to resolve them.
